# Decode-one-instruction drill — 2026-11-27

Target: Windows x86-64.

A function gets a pointer-sized address whose low 32 bits are in `ecx`. You see:

```
81 E1 FF 0F 00 00
```

Decode it. If `ecx` holds `0x7FF6A123`, what's in `ecx` afterwards? And why didn't the compiler use the shorter `83` form you saw back on 2026-09-06?

---

## Answer

`and ecx, 0FFFh`. Afterwards `ecx` = **`0x123`**. In C that's `addr & 0xFFF`, the byte offset inside a 4 KB page.

- `81` is the Group-1 opcode with a full **imm32**. ModRM `E1` = `11 100 001`: mod=11 means register-direct, reg=100 is the extension **/4 = AND** (/0 ADD, /1 OR, /5 SUB, /7 CMP), and rm=001 is ecx. The four bytes `FF 0F 00 00` are read little-endian as `0x00000FFF`.
- `83` stores a single byte and sign-extends it, so it can only encode -128..127. `0xFFF` doesn't fit, so the compiler needs the 4-byte immediate. A mask like `-16` (`0xFFFFFFF0`) does fit, which is why the align-down idiom is just `83 E1 F0`.
- AND keeps only the bits that are 1 in the mask, here the low 12. `0x7FF6A123 & 0xFFF` = `0x123`.

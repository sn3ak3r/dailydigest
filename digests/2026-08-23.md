## Deep Digest 1: Windows Named Pipes — Local Does Not Mean Trusted

**Problem:** Named pipes let Windows processes talk to each other locally, but a workstation runs code under many privilege levels at once — LocalSystem, admins, standard users, services — and any process that knows a pipe's name can try to connect to it.

**Approach, in plain words:** The writeup catalogs how attackers plant pipes with predictable names, spoof their identity, or send malformed messages to hijack a listener — then lays out defenses: strict per-pipe access lists, verifying identity on connect, authorizing every command, and blocking remote clients.

**Result:** A 15-point hardening checklist spanning access control, input validation, impersonation handling, and resource limits.

**Why it matters:** A LocalSystem service exposed over a poorly secured pipe is a direct route to privilege escalation or denial of service — a well-known but commonly mishandled attack surface.

**Jargon glossary:**
- **Named pipe** — a Windows IPC channel two processes connect to by a shared name.
- **DACL** — Discretionary Access Control List; the permissions attached to a pipe controlling who can connect.
- **Impersonation** — a server temporarily acting with a connecting client's privileges to serve a request.
- **Confused deputy** — a privileged process tricked into misusing its authority on an attacker's behalf.

## Deep Digest 2: Iron Hydride Goes Superionic Deep in Earth's Core

**Problem:** Simulations have long suggested iron hydride turns "superionic" — a solid metal lattice with hydrogen flowing through it like a liquid — under the pressure and heat of Earth's inner core, but nobody had confirmed it experimentally.

**Approach, in plain words:** Researchers squeezed iron hydride to core-like pressures (50–110 GPa) and heated it past 2,000 Kelvin, watching the crystal with X-rays while running current through it to track hydrogen movement.

**Result:** Near 1,590 Kelvin — below actual core temperatures — the material showed a telltale superionic transition, with hydrogen mobile enough to flow through the fixed lattice, yet slow enough to move only ~0.1 micrometers per 10,000 years.

**Why it matters:** First direct evidence of superionic behavior in iron-hydrogen alloys, helping explain seismic-wave anomalies in the inner core and showing hydrogen from Earth's formation stays trapped there for eons.

**Jargon glossary:**
- **Superionic state** — a solid whose heavy atoms stay fixed while light atoms move through it like a liquid.
- **Diamond-anvil cell** — a device that compresses tiny samples between diamond tips to extreme pressures.
- **Gigapascal (GPa)** — a unit of pressure; 1 GPa is roughly 10,000 atmospheres.
- **Synchrotron X-ray diffraction** — using intense X-rays to map a sample's crystal structure in real time.

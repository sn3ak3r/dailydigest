## Deep Digest 1: Does Computer Science Need Computers?

**Problem:** Is computer science its own branch of mathematics, or is it inseparable from the physical machines it's named after?

**Approach, in plain words:** Quanta traces the field's split origins in math and engineering — Turing worked out his model of computation in 1937, a decade before real computers existed, purely to answer a math question — against cases where deep theoretical questions, especially in complexity theory, only got asked once real machines made them urgent.

**Result:** Theory can run independent of hardware, but historically the best theoretical questions came from problems engineers actually ran into.

**Why it matters:** It reframes CS as a two-way street: practice doesn't just apply theory, it generates the questions theory ends up needing to answer.

**Jargon:** *Complexity theory* — the study of how much time/memory a problem inherently requires to solve. *Model of computation* — a formal, math-only definition of what "computing" means, independent of any physical machine. *Algorithm* — a precise step-by-step procedure for solving a problem.

## Deep Digest 2: Self-Blinking Fairy Lights Image DNA Near Its True Width

**Problem:** Standard microscopy blurs anything under 200nm, and the chemicals/lasers needed for higher resolution kill living cells — so DNA's fine structure has mostly only been seen in dead tissue.

**Approach, in plain words:** Researchers built fluorescent probes ("HoTs") that seek out and bind DNA inside living cells while flickering like fairy lights. That blinking lets super-resolution methods (STORM, MINFLUX) reconstruct sharp images from many dim, staggered flashes. An AI tool, AINU, then reads subtle chromatin-folding patterns in the results.

**Result:** STORM hit 20nm resolution in living cells; MINFLUX reached 3nm in preserved cells, close to the double helix's actual 2nm width. In bowel cancer biopsies, tumor DNA was visibly looser and more spread out than healthy tissue, and AINU distinguished cell types by chromatin pattern with 96–98% accuracy.

**Why it matters:** DNA packing density could become a new, fast diagnostic signal for cancer aggressiveness, layered on top of century-old tissue staining.

**Jargon:** *Super-resolution microscopy* — techniques that beat the ~200nm blur limit of ordinary light microscopes. *Chromatin* — DNA packaged together with proteins inside the cell nucleus. *STORM/MINFLUX* — two super-resolution imaging methods that localize individual blinking fluorescent molecules. *Double helix* — DNA's two-strand spiral structure, about 2nm wide.
